Situation

Why this mattered

Inviting a teammate is also a decision about access

When an admin invites a teammate to Pipedrive, they are also deciding what that person can see and what they can do. In practice, many admins skipped that decision. Visibility and permissions were confusing enough that people took the defaults, or never looked at them.

At the same time, Pipedrive was moving toward an agentic direction for onboarding. That made this the right moment to rethink how a new team gets set up, not only how the screen looks.

The invite itself was never the hard part. The hard part is everything the admin has to figure out around it.

What admins told us

The friction starts after the invite

I ran discovery with admins and drew on interviews, recordings and notes gathered for the project. A few patterns stood out.

Inviting is easy

Every admin found adding a teammate fast. The friction starts after the invite.

Admins become the support desk

They train, troubleshoot and answer questions for their team, often without using Pipedrive’s own help.

No way to see engagement

Admins had no reliable signal in the product for whether a teammate was getting started.

Two different problems

Some admins truly weren’t sure how to structure access. Others knew exactly what they wanted, for reasons no interface could change.

Based on a small set of admin interviews. These are early patterns, not statistics.

Task

What I set out to do

Three goals, set together with Kaur

Design and prototype

A Team Onboarding flow that helps admins invite teammates and set sensible permissions and visibility.

Validate

The flow with Customer Success and with real users.

Simplify the language

So admins aren’t asked to learn product terms before they can finish setup.

Action

What we did

Understand the problem from the inside

I asked a teammate who knows the system well to walk me through permissions and visibility groups, since I needed to understand how they work before I could simplify them.

I then asked the Customer Success team what customers ask them, what information they need from an admin, and whether they use scripts or checklists. Their answers shaped what the flow asks and in what order. I mapped the whole flow in a Lucid document so the team could review it together.

Prototype, review, repeat

Four rounds of prototyping and review

I built the prototypes in Lovable so stakeholders could click through real flows instead of reading about them.

  1. Narrowed the scope

    After the second feedback round I removed the “how many users” step so the flow starts from emails. I added a chat that shows people what happens with their answers, and a step for picking the tasks on the Get started page. The first slices included no AI.

    Round 1

  2. Focused on the invite

    The admin picks emails, roles and goals. They then see pre-selected permissions, a welcome message, and a preview of what the invitee will see.

    Round 2

  3. Agentic prototype and two experiments

    I built the agentic onboarding prototype and presented it to leadership. I also explored a “What can this user see” side panel and an “Invite users” entry in the new navigation, where I moved the button to the top because the user table was already crowded.

    Round 3

  4. Sharpened the questions and the UI

    I refined which questions lead to a meaningful final setup. I aligned the interface with the agent being built by another team, so admins wouldn’t meet two different environments. I also proposed asking, right after the invitations, whether to configure access now or keep the defaults.

    Round 4

The flow, in short

Five steps from emails to access

  1. Add emailsStart from who you’re inviting.
  2. Pick roles and goalsWhat will each person do?
  3. Review pre-selected accessSensible permissions, already set.
  4. Preview and sendWelcome message, plus what the invitee will see.
  5. Configure now or keep defaultsAdmins choose how much detail they want.

Simplified from the prototype.

Vocabulary

Question the vocabulary

Customer Success describe these settings as “what users can see” and “what users can do”. Our product says “visibility” and “permissions”. I asked why we use the product terms at all, took it to content design, and opened a research ticket to trace the history of the terms and find simpler ones.

Validation

Plan validation

I set up a user testing plan with a researcher: one guided task (invite a finance teammate and a sales teammate with given permissions) and one open task. I wanted to rework the last step of the flow before testing started. Throughout, I kept product, design and leadership stakeholders in the loop.

Result

Where it stands

A clear direction, and the material to move forward

The flow hasn’t been built yet, so there are no measured results to report. What we did deliver is a clear direction and the material to move forward with confidence.

Four prototypes

The agentic flow, the invite flow, the “What can this user see” panel, and the navigation entry.

A mapped flow

The full journey in one document for the team to review.

Customer Success input

Real questions from the people who explain this to customers every day.

A terminology ticket

Research into simpler words for visibility and permissions.

A testing plan

Ready to validate with real users.

The flow now starts from pre-made permission and visibility settings, so admins begin with a sensible setup instead of a blank page.

Looking ahead

What I’d measure, and what I learned

What I’d measure next

Once the flow is built, I’d look at how many admins customize access instead of skipping it, how quickly invited teammates become active, and how many setup questions reach support.

What I learned

Prototyping early made the conversations with stakeholders concrete, and the research changed how we framed the problem: part of “permission confusion” is a design problem, and part isn’t. Not every slow rollout is product friction, so the flow aims to remove the confusion we can solve and to stay out of the way for the rest.
